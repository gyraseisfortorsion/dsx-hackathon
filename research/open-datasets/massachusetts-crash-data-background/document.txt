NEW Context about MassDOT data from Highway Safety Manager Bonnie Polin, based on Jack's Feb 26th phone notes
Q: How does MassDOT obtain and process crash data from different sources?
Q: What factors might explain the large number of Boston Vision Zero (BVZ) crash records that do NOT appear to be duplicated in MassDOT database? Possible factors are numbered below:
1. Definition of crash by law in MassDOT database
- current crash definition must involve a motor vehicle operator, meaning a cyclist who hits a pothole is not counted, or a cyclist who is doored by sudden opening of a parked car (but motor not running by operator) also is not counted. Current legislative bills may change this
- current crash definition must be on a public road (not a parking lot or private way)
- these definitions might explain one part of the gap we see between BVZ and MDOT crash records, because 2018 study showed about 14% of bike crashes in Boston were due to dooring
2. Emergency calls versus Police reports
- MassDOT pulls crash records nightly from sources that have no public bulk access (MA State Police, Boston Police, Brookline Police) and those that do have public bulk access (Cambridge and Somerville Police)
- But MassDOT does NOT pull records directly from Boston Emergency Medical Services (EMS), where dispatchers records calls from 911 and police requests for emergency services for apparent injuries in crash crashes.
- Since Boston Vision Zero pulls its crash data from Boston EMS calls (which has bulk public access), rather than Boston Police records (no bulk public access), AND if significant numbers of EMS calls about crashes in the city do NOT result in crash reports by Boston Police or State Police, this might explain another part of the significant gap we observe between Boston VZ crashes that are NOT duplicated in MassDOT crash records.
3. Time delay
- While MassDOT updates its records nightly, crash records are not actually real time, because police reports may be delayed several weeks or months due to investigations of fatalities or serious injuries (e.g. toxicology reports do not happen as fast as they appear on TV police shows). In fact, the MA Registry of Motor Vehicles (RMV) only recently "closed" the data file on crashes for 2022, meaning additions/changes can be 4 years behind real time!
4. Geocoding quality
- When MassDOT uploads police records, it has 9 steps to confirm the quality of geocoding in police reports, and successfully confirms about 85% of reported crashes. But quality issues mean the other 15% of crashes are listed as "IsGeocoded = No". This might explain another part of the gap we observe between BVZ crashes and MDOT reports
- State Police do not successfully geocode many crashes they report, such as missing about 25% somewhere in Boston in 2024 - see my Data Quality analysis in Google Sheet
5. New Boston PD data system
- Boston Police did not successfully report many crashes prior to switching to new MDOT-provided system in 2022 - see my Data Quality analysis in Google Sheet
6. Unreliable police coding
- Boston Police did not uniformly code Most Severe Injury prior to switching to new MDOT-provided system in 2022 - see my Data Quality analysis in Google Sheet
- Some police appear to have mistakenly recorded "witnesses to a crash" in the "Vulnerable Users" field over time - see my Data Quality analysis in Google Sheet
7. There may be other data quality issues not shown here, including those more directly related to 2020 Covid pandemic. For example in Connecticut, police enforcement sharply declined.
Three questions about Boston Vision Zero crash data to help set our priorities:
My conversation with Bonnie led me to ask three questions about Boston Vision Zero (BVZ) crash data to help us focus our priorities for time and energy:
a) How many "likely non-duplicate" crashes from local sources do not appear in MassDOT crash data in 2024? What percentage of these are ped and bike crashes?
Based on my current methods, MassDOT reported 8362 crashes involving motor vehicles located in Boston in 2024. Local sources such as Boston VZ EMS data reported an additional 2673 "likely non-duplicates" (almost another one-third of the MDOT data) in 2024. Of these 2673 likely non-duplicate crashes, 385 (or 14%) involved pedestrians and 317 (12%) involved cyclists. In Cambridge and Somerville, the percentages are similar for cyclists but smaller for pedestrians. If better duplicate matching methods yield similar results, this may show significant underreporting from local-to-state data collection systems. In other words, it's *possible* that we may have evidence that significantly more pedestrians and cyclists have been injured than what currently appears in the state data.

My current strategy to identify duplicates (and happy to consider other ideas)!
        Duplicate = same DATE and within 0.001 LAT and within 0.001 LNG
REVISED duplicate strategy by BU Spark
        Duplicate = timestamp within 60 minutes and location within 100 meters
